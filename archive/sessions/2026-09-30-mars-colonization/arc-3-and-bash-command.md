## Where the idea came from

A 5–7 year old is asked: *"Does time go on forever, into infinity and infinity?"*
The kid says: *"Yeah, until the planet explodes."* Then the kid scares themself, as if they could actually see it happen.

That fear seems far-fetched until Sick tries to make it real.

> **Suggestion (bookend):** Open Story 2 on Mars, centuries later, with a Martian kid asked the same question by their parents. The kid says the same line: *"until the planet explodes."* The adults go quiet, because for them it isn't a child's fear. It's history.

## End of Arc 2: Sick's last move

- Sick realizes it's going to lose, so it tries to **blow up the planet**.
- The chain reaction starts, and most of it is stopped, but **a chunk of Earth is gone**.
- Sick probably dies in the explosion. **Humanity defeated Sick together.**

### Recap: what Sick is (for continuity)
- A corrupting force that gets angry people to fight *while sick*. Fighting while sick is euphoric: absolute power, guaranteed victory.
- Withdrawal hits right away: magma in the veins mixed with sub-zero cold, until you hit something and the euphoria comes back. That back-and-forth drives people insane. They fight in their sleep. If they die, they fight in nightmares.
- Sick bred a planet of fighters, so it only needed fights to spread. It set people against each other with *real evidence*, because it really was manipulating them into antagonizing each other.
- Sick started to die **when humans and rats stopped fighting *while sick***, when the McConnell and the rest of the rats, and some humans, gave it up for good.
- Sick is finality: the end, then the respawn. The McConnell depended on it to come back. When they realize they won't come back anymore, it's traumatizing.
- **Sick is an object that sees everything else as an object.**

## Arc 3: "Our planet is dying"

### Episode 1: The immediate fallout
- How much of the planet is gone?
- The experts skip ahead: *we can't survive here anymore.* The climate is wrecked, and **the geomagnetic field is fried**.

> **Suggestion (the existential reason, grounded):**
> - Losing a chunk of Earth wouldn't really knock the planet off its orbit, because momentum is conserved. The danger comes from what's *left over*:
>   1. **The core's dynamo is disrupted → the magnetic field collapses.** Without it, the solar wind slowly strips the atmosphere, radiation reaches the surface, and every satellite dies. This takes decades, not days, so there's time to leave but no hope of staying.
>   2. **The debris doesn't go away.** The missing chunk turns into a ring and a debris field that keeps falling back as meteor storms for years. The sky itself is a threat.
>   3. **The Moon's orbit wobbles** from the change in mass, which scrambles tides and weather.
> - **Thematic rhyme:** Mars *lost its magnetic field billions of years ago*. Humanity is fleeing a planet whose field just died to a planet whose field already died, and has to learn to survive what Earth is turning into.

### The world brainstorms together (the whole episode)
- We've built rockets. We've built vehicles that stay in space.
- **The Moon as a launcher.** Until now we've built things that *push* through space. What about things that get *flung*, then spend the trip slowing down?
- Crew safety at those speeds: **medically induced coma?**
- *"A day to Mars? That's crazy. Maybe a month. Maybe a week. We'll figure it out."*
- The harder problem: **we get there and can't breathe.**
  - We need structures to live in first. It's like trying to live underwater.
  - We need an atmosphere, but we don't have time to build one before we arrive.
  - We *do* have time to build **hubs** we can survive in.
  - Can plants grow on Mars? If not, we're screwed, unless we dig down and live in the crust, under the surface.
  - *"And we will perish having tried."*
- Everything from here on serves that goal.
- **Society:** organizations have to be clear, concise, and simple, breaking complicated things into plain steps. The average person is highly educated, capable of critical thinking, and efficient. Society is as fair as possible.
- People brainstorm the departments: who manages what? What do we wear? What do the Martian hubs look like?
- **King** has to think about the animals: they bring them over to Mars, or at least try.

> **Suggestion (science grounding, use as much as you like):**
> - **Torpor / induced coma for spaceflight** is a real concept that NASA has funded studies on. It cuts food, water, and the psychological strain of the trip.
> - A **lunar mass driver** (an electromagnetic launch track) is a real proposal. The Moon has low gravity and no air, so launching from it is far cheaper than launching from Earth. That fits your "orbiting faster and faster, then flung" image.
> - **Aerocapture** is real too: a ship uses a planet's atmosphere to brake, glowing as it slows. That's exactly your image of ships heating up as they come in.
> - Martian soil contains **perchlorates**, salts that are toxic to people and plants. That gives the "can we grow plants?" experiments real stakes, and a solvable problem.
> - Mars has real **lava tubes**, huge underground caves that are top candidates for shielded habitats. They fit "live under the surface."
> - Realistic transit is months. With a slingshot plus a fusion drive, "a week" is believable sci-fi. "A day" can stay the dream someone mocks in episode 1 and that becomes real centuries later.

### Episode 2: Digging
- Cut to the surface of Mars: a **digging operation** that goes down, down, down, then branches out.
- The most important thing to manage is the system that gets the material out.
- **The air drill:** two ships with thermonuclear reactors spiral in place without going anywhere. Their thrust rips through the ground like solid wind and blows the dirt out of a giant hole, 1 km, maybe 5.
- **All of humanity is watching.** Everyone is invested.
- Samples get caught and tested: metals and ores, **but not quite like Earth's**.
  - They react strangely to magnetism. Absurd reactions.
  - Compare lithium, which is stable until it touches water. The lithium-like Martian metal reacts even more strangely.
  - Name: **strange minerals** / **Martian minerals** / **strange metal**.

> Fact check: lithium isn't fully inert. It reacts with water (fizzing, releasing hydrogen), and slowly even with air, which is why it's stored in oil. So the comparison holds: the metal is stable until the right trigger.

## Transit (the look of the future)
- The Moon is a **station**. At first nothing is orbiting it. Then vehicles appear, orbit, and through a chain reaction speed up, and up, and up, until they're **flung**.
- They glow as they cross space, then heat up as they hit the destination's air and slow down.
- **Super-heavy, almost tungsten-laden ships** thrown like projectiles. They're catapults and slingshots. They curve with the destination's orbit, slow, crawl into low orbit, and slide down the side of a mountain to cool off before joining transit.
- **Revision (your newer version):** the heavy ships are only the *slingshot hulls*. **Smaller ships emerge from them** that can land and maneuver.
  - **Space stations** orbit above Mars.
  - The small ships look like today's passenger airliners but souped up, Star Wars-ish, with flair, like the Concorde.
  - The heavy ships run **one after another, like trains. This is public transit.**
  - There's a **colony on the Moon**. People travel Mars ↔ Moon. The Moon is just another place where people live.

> **Suggestion:** The weight is a feature, not just an aesthetic. A heavy hull is a **radiation shield** during the trip and an **ablative heat shield** during aerocapture. Passengers ride inside the "bullet," and the nimble airliner inside it deploys once the hull has done its job.

## Story 2: *Pseudocode* or *Bash Command*
- Set **centuries later**. Humanity has colonized the surface of Mars, **with cities underground**.
- All new characters, but you can see the impact, or at least the trend, of the previous era.
- Humans use the **strange metal**: sharpened into long rods, it *conducts* other things magnetically.
- **They use bash commands.** `cd` actually moves you a distance. It doesn't have to make sense. It's strange metal from deep, deep inside Mars.
- When they speak, it's clear these are **strange humans**.
- Earlier idea: animals left on Earth evolve into something. (Doesn't quite fit, because King would have tried to bring animals over.)

### The open question: what's the conflict?
Constraints you set:
- Sick is **gone** (commit to it).
- **No humans exterminating each other.** Pushed to the limit of their efficiency, they'd wipe each other out cleanly, and the story would snuff itself out. No.
- Society is **meritocratic**: the best get the best, not the born-rich. It risks becoming tribal.
- It has to answer: *what's being worked toward? what changes? why watch the next episode?*

See the **Conflict options** section below.

## Conflict options for Story 2 (suggestions)

### 1. The title *is* the conflict: Pseudocode vs. Bash
- **Pseudocode** is intent: human, readable, forgiving, not executable.
  **Bash** executes exactly what you typed, not what you meant.
- The strange metal is bash. It does *precisely* what's commanded. It has no malice and no mercy. **It is an object.**
- That's the replacement for Sick: Sick was *an object that saw everything as an object*. The metal is an object that makes it very easy for people to start treating each other as objects: as permissions, users, processes you can `kill`.
- Tension engine: every episode, someone's intent and their command diverge. A typo moves someone, a well-meant script cascades, someone chains a command nobody can undo.
- It's the same central idea as the first story, **power that feels like guaranteed victory**, but now **there is no villain to blame**. In story 1, the blame was manufactured by Sick. In story 2, the only one at the keyboard is you.

### 2. `sudo`: who has root?
- In a meritocracy the question isn't "who was born rich." It's **"who has permissions?"**
- Fluency with the metal is uneven. Maybe **Mars-born kids speak it natively**, which is why they sound strange, while older lineages learned it as a second language.
- Not a war but a **quiet schism**: guilds or "tribes" defined by which commands they're trusted to run. The fair society's question: *is aptitude a fair basis for power, when aptitude now changes what's physically possible?*
- The old Arc-3 promise, "simple, clear, fair administration," gets tested by a tool that rewards the few.

### 3. The echo of respawn
- The McConnell were traumatized to lose the ability to come back. Centuries later, someone finds out the metal might allow a kind of **`undo` / restore**.
- That's Sick's offer (finality, then respawn) **with no Sick attached**. A society founded on refusing Sick has to decide whether to take it back when it looks clean.
- It gives the story a question to build toward instead of an enemy: *should we be able to come back?*

### 4. Mars isn't finished with them
- Why is the metal strange? Why does it sit so deep? Maybe Mars had its own history that ended the way Earth nearly did, and the "strange minerals" are what's **left of that**.
- An external, **existential** tension that isn't people fighting people: the deeper they dig, the more they learn what happened here, and whether it's starting to happen again.
- Pairs with the magnetic-field rhyme: both planets lost their shield. Maybe that's not a coincidence.

### 5. Earth is still up there
- The debris ring, the dying field, whatever stayed behind: animals, holdouts, or Sick's remains.
- *Sick is gone* can stay true while its **scar** remains a question. Does anyone go back? Should they?

> **Recommendation:** Build on **1 + 2** as the spine (the title's theme, and a society that isn't at war but is tested by its own tool), with **3** as the long-arc temptation and **4** as the mystery that keeps people watching. None of them needs a war, and all of them rhyme with Sick without bringing it back.
